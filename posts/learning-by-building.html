Ten Years of Not Writing It Down

April 2026 · 8 min read

I have built microservice frameworks other engineers depended on, taken a distributed CI system from 80,000 builds a month to over 400,000, and led a team through the growth that made that necessary. I have debugged a build scheduler at 2am and explained to a room of stakeholders why infrastructure work that ships nothing visible was the right quarter to spend. In roughly a decade of doing this, the number of those lessons I wrote down anywhere someone else could read is approximately zero.

That is a strange thing to notice about yourself. The knowledge is not gone — it is in my hands, in the instinct that says this will page someone in six months. But instinct does not transfer, and it does not survive me changing jobs. This blog is the correction.

Here is the short version of where the instinct came from, and the one thread I only saw when I lined it all up.

Bologna: The Vocabulary

I started computer science at the University of Bologna in 2010 and left with a Bachelor's and a Master's, 110/110 cum laude, with a thesis on microservice architectures — back when microservices were a contested idea rather than the default answer.

University gave me the vocabulary and the mental models, and I do not undersell that: knowing the name of a thing is what lets you look it up at 2am. What it did not give me was any feel for how those models bend under real constraints — a deadline, a legacy system, a team that disagrees. That gap between understanding something and making it work became the running theme of everything after.

Ferrero and Reply: Building for Other Engineers

My first professional taste was an internship at Ferrero in Luxembourg in 2016: my introduction to how software actually gets built inside a large organisation. The design docs. The meetings that could have been emails. The permanent negotiation between what is elegant and what ships this quarter.

Then Milan, and Reply, on a project with CheBanca! that landed squarely in my thesis territory — building frameworks so the wider engineering department could stand up microservices quickly, safely and consistently. Infrastructure work with a product mindset, because the users were engineers and there were dozens of them depending on our abstractions.

That is where school stopped being the reference point:

The hardest part of building software is not the code. It is making the code work for people who were not in the room when you designed it.

An abstraction that only its author can use is not an abstraction. It is a private joke with an API.

Dublin and Murex: Learning by Explaining

The Reply stint was short, because I wanted to be abroad again. I landed in Dublin at Murex, a leader in capital markets software, on the risk control team — real scrum, real ownership of features end to end, production support rotations, maintenance, mentoring.

The part I am proudest of is the innovation sprints. Murex gave engineers allocated time to explore new technology and pitch ideas to the company. I used them to prototype things I thought could matter, and some of them did.

But the durable lesson was smaller and more uncomfortable: I learned more in the hours I spent articulating my thinking to someone else than in the weeks I spent quietly writing code. Mentoring, pitching, defending a technical decision to someone who was not convinced — every one of those forces you to find the part of your reasoning you had been skipping. Writing code lets you keep the hand-wave. Explaining it does not.

HubSpot: Scaling the Build

I joined HubSpot in 2020, mid-COVID, and met my team in person more than a year after my start date. The company was growing fast — the pandemic had accelerated the shift to digital — and that growth was pressing on everything, including the build system.

My team owned HubSpot's internal CI: a fully in-house distributed system, originally built at speed by a small group who also owned deployment. Impressive engineering under time pressure, and like all such engineering, built on assumptions. By the time I arrived the Maven dependency tree had grown deeper than those assumptions allowed, build volume was climbing, and critical incidents were frequent.

Over two and a half years I went from Software Engineer to Senior to Tech Lead. We took the system from roughly 80,000 builds a month to over 400,000, while decreasing critical incidents and cutting artifact storage costs, and I grew the team from three to five including an intern rotation.

The numbers are the part that fits on a CV. The lessons were all in how we got there: debugging distributed failures in the middle of the night, trading throughput against isolation knowing both choices would hurt somewhere, convincing stakeholders that a quarter with no visible features was the right investment, and leading a team through problems where nobody in the room knew the answer yet. None of which I wrote down.

Metrics, and the Thing I Got Wrong

In June 2023 I moved back to Italy for personal reasons and stayed with HubSpot, remote. The change of country coincided with a change of problem: I joined the DevEx Metrics team.

The original mandate came from the DORA paper — four metrics that were supposed to tell you how well an engineering organisation was doing. It took us very little time to conclude that they did not, at least not on their own, so we charted our own path and built an application that surfaced engineering productivity insights at three granularities: individual, team, leadership.

It worked. People used it heavily, and teams genuinely saw things about themselves they could not see before. Which is exactly when it became a problem.

Scar

Some managers started using our numbers in performance reviews. Nobody asked us, and nothing in the product stopped them. In theory it sounds defensible — you have data, why not use it. In practice a metric stripped of its managerial context is just a number, and numbers make terrible judges of people. We had built something powerful and shipped it without deciding what it was not for. That is a design decision, and declining to make it does not mean it goes unmade; it means your users make it for you.

That tension between the quantitative and the qualitative is what led to the work I am most proud of. For HubSpot's internal hackathon last year I pitched an idea: instead of only showing metrics, what if we could describe engineering productivity in human terms? I formed a team, merged with another team chasing a complementary vision, and we built an application that consumed data we already had and generated qualitative narratives about an individual's engineering work.

We won by a wide margin over second place, which was fun, and largely beside the point. What mattered is what happened next: my team shipped it to production for everyone at HubSpot, then evolved it into an agentic system producing narratives good enough that some of the most respected engineers in the company wrote to us unprompted to say so.

The Thread

Lining those up, the work I am proudest of is all the same shape, and I did not notice for years.

Each role contributed a piece. Bologna gave me the vocabulary. Ferrero showed me how enterprises actually decide things. Reply taught me to build for engineers who were not in the room. Murex taught me that explaining a thing is how you find out whether you understand it. HubSpot made me an engineer who can hold a distributed system in my head and a lead who can hold a team through ambiguity. The metrics work taught me that shipping something powerful without deciding what it is not for is itself a decision.

Why Write

So: this blog does two things. It reflects on what I have learned, because the instinct is worth more written down than carried around. And it documents what I am still learning, which matters just as much — a decade in, I am still building side projects, still hitting walls, still having the "wait, how does this actually work?" moment at eleven at night.

There is also the selfish reason, and it is the one Murex taught me: I do not fully know what I think about a system until I have had to explain it to somebody. Writing is the cheapest way to force that, and unlike a mentoring session, the artifact outlives the conversation.

Expect technical deep dives, honest post-mortems, the occasional "I wasted a weekend on this and here is the actual cause", and practical takes on what worked and what did not — including the scars, because those are the only part of the story that is genuinely hard to get anywhere else.
